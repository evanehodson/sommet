The worst piece of country conceivable.

In January of 1890 the Press party spent nine days crossing what a modern runner covers in an afternoon — chest-deep snow, failing rations, and a line no white party had recorded before them. Their diary does not flatter the terrain.

The divide itself sits at 3,667 feet, unremarkable by Cascade standards, but it is the hinge of the whole traverse: everything before it drains toward the Elwha, everything after toward the Quinault. Runners feel the switch underfoot before they see it on a map.

From the 5,018-foot saddle at Press Point to the ravine floor at The Slot, the course keeps the original party’s discipline — no vehicles, no resupply beyond what a crew can walk in, and aid stations set where the old camps stood.

Aid Station 4 sits at the same saddle today, staffed, stocked, and reachable by crew — the one mercy the modern course grants that the original party never had. Six checkpoints, fifty miles, one daylight.
